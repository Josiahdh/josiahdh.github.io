Featured at GDC 2026, *Masterworks of Horror: The Prologue* is a real-time PvP card-battler created in USC’s Advanced Games Project program with a team of over 55 students and volunteers.

## Production Seen Through Trailers
My role on Masterworks was managing and leading our large team from recruitment to publishing alongside our creative director. I led production: scheduling, sprint tracking, notes, documentation, team structure, and more. But with my [interdisciplinary design and film background](/about/), I quickly became the team creative Swiss Army knife, handling slide deck formatting, fonts, trailers, and more. Creating trailers became my natural rhythm of seeing where the game was at, not just internally, but how others might see what we had to showcase.

{% include gallery-video-grid.html videos=page.trailer-versions wide=true active_index=3 %}

The trend of development was moving from conceptually cool to clear and impactful. Our first Fall Recruitment Trailer was a core team-wide push to showcase our game as best as possible in comparison to other projects students could choose to work on. We had just wrapped up a summer of preproduction, and were still making tweaks to the game up until the trailer was exported. Narrative became my strategy here: I experimented with some AI animation and recruited a voice actor friend of mine to supplement our gameplay’s good atmosphere but unclear interactions.

Arguably the largest shift was between our Fall and Spring Recruitment Trailer. Almost immediately after hitting a true vertical slice and pausing most development in December, we decided to make our portrait game a landscape one. The refactor was handled by the core team, and soon it was time to pitch our game again. While AI animation and voiceover were still there, visual and narrative cohesion was improved. I began experimenting with customized After Effects templates to create animated card art segments. We also began finding the gameplay recording pipeline that would be used throughout the rest of development using OBS and mobile screen recording. And, to bring it all together, I cut the trailer to our original game soundtrack.

![GDC-1](/assets/images/masterworks/gdc-1.JPG){:.w-50.float-right}

GDC was when trailers got fun. I could direct a proper narrative because we had the art for entire authors’ decks. AI animation was replaced with After Effects sequences. We built a free-cam tool that I designed, allowing for more cinematic interactions. Visuals got a huge overhaul, requiring me to rerecord footage far too close to release. One of my favorite memories working on Masterworks was recording gameplay footage in the basement of the San Francisco Moscone Center before the GDC Show Floor had opened while many of my friends were in sessions. While I was not at talks, I certainly learned from hands-on gameplay capture! In addition to the GDC Trailer, I edited a tutorial gameplay loop shown at our GDC booth.

In between my third and fourth trailers, we produced an [outsourced trailer](https://youtu.be/j5JYjRB4w18?si=3pDgwZ-py7tCe5_3) that I storyboarded and recorded gameplay for. Development made it a rocky time, as bugs meant we often had to rerecord gameplay after patches. My not being allowed to edit this trailer built up in me a desire to showcase the project once and for all.

The Prologue trailer cemented the fork in development: Masterworks would live on to be a bigger IP while *Masterworks of Horror: The Prologue* would be the version we produced with USC and released. This final trailer was my send-off, a way to encompass the project I had produced and a way to properly feature some of our Trapped Authors’ cards that had been completed after GDC.

Throughout development, I learned trailers are massive morale boosters for a team. They also provide an excellent opportunity to check in across departments, provide feedback, and assess where a game is at in production.

## Creative Producing
Part of the fun of being a creative producer is getting to suggest ideas with the understanding of where a team is at in development, making your suggestions more easily accepted. After we had hit beta, I pitched the design for a meta-progression quest system to add ~5 hours of content to the game. The way I saw it, meta-progression was free content, given it was simply tracking how a player played. It also gave some direction for players to learn different strategies, mechanics, and combos. Before I knew it, the idea went from mine to the team’s.

I used to not enjoy the idea of being a producer because it meant you did not have a creative voice. But it is precisely the overarching, interdisciplinary view that a producer must have that allowed me to support a creative vision and utilize my design background. On Masterworks, I regularly provided design, art, UI, and marketing feedback. My opinion was not just heard, it was sought after, which was a joy. 

Ultimately, I believe I was a better fit in the lead producer role. Initially, I was going to be a designer on Masterworks, but I welcomed the invitation to produce because I felt I did not have the specific vision for (card) design that the designer needed. Instead, my role was supporting the creative direction of my friends and teammates. And naturally, as creative people do, I developed some ideas of my own along the way and was blessed with a team that valued my creative voice.
